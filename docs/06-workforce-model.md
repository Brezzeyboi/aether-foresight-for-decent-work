# AETHER — The Workforce Transformation Model

**Compiled:** August 2026
**Inputs:** [`04-research-evidence.md`](04-research-evidence.md) (100 verified claims), [`05-sources.md`](05-sources.md) (24 sources).
**Purpose:** Turn the evidence base into a structured model of workforce change across 2026 → 2030 → 2035 → 2040 → 2045, with the certainty of every statement made explicit.

---

## 0. The epistemic spine

The single organising fact of this model: **nothing in the evidence base forecasts 2045** (CLAIM-SCEN-01). The furthest rigorous horizons located are WEF's employer-survey extrapolation to 2030 (CLAIM-EMP-01 through CLAIM-EMP-04), BLS's modelled US projections to 2034 (CLAIM-JOB-07), and Acemoglu's ten-year macro window (CLAIM-ECON-01). The longest horizon found anywhere was union foresight roadmaps to 2040, and those are explicitly non-predictive scenario exercises (CLAIM-SCEN-08).

So the model does not extend a forecast. It degrades certainty in four steps, and the language changes with each step. This is not modesty for its own sake; the ILO states outright that generative AI's development cannot be predicted (CLAIM-SCEN-02), asks not to be quoted for headline figures because the value of its work is *directional* (CLAIM-SCEN-03), and distinguishes foresight from forecasting as a matter of method (CLAIM-SCEN-08). The IMF characterises AI's economic implications as carrying uncertainty "reminiscent of past introductions of general-purpose technologies, such as electricity" (CLAIM-ECON-07). Acemoglu labels his own central figures upper bounds built on assumptions he calls speculative (CLAIM-SCEN-10). Copying that register is the defensible choice; inventing 2045 numbers is not (CLAIM-SCEN-13).

| Horizon | Basis in force | Verb register | What the model is doing |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2026 | `measured` | is, has, was | Reporting observed data |
| 2030 | `projection` | is projected to, employers expect | Reporting others' published projections |
| 2035 | extrapolation (no basis class — flagged as ours) | would, on current direction of travel | Reasoning one step past the last projection |
| 2040 | `scenario` | could, if | Conditional pathway |
| 2045 | `scenario` | could, if — and only comparatively | Conditional pathway, directional outcomes only |

Two structural asymmetries run through every horizon and should be read as permanent features of the model, not as caveats:

1. **The datasets describe a minority of the world's workers.** WEF's base is 1.2 billion *formal* jobs (CLAIM-EMP-04) while 2.1 billion workers are expected to hold informal jobs in 2026 (CLAIM-EMP-09). BLS is US-only (CLAIM-JOB-07). ILO WP140's worker survey is Polish, 1,640 respondents, with clerical workers and managers over-represented (CLAIM-AUG-09). OECD findings are OECD-country findings (CLAIM-SKILL-10). Any statement about "the workforce" in this model means, unless said otherwise, the formal-sector workforce that these instruments can see.

2. **Exposure is not job loss.** IMF's ~40% (CLAIM-EMP-05) and ILO's one-in-four (CLAIM-AUG-04) measure task overlap with model capability computed on occupational structures. They are not counts of jobs at risk and not predictions of unemployment. No source in the evidence base provides a measured count of jobs actually lost to AI anywhere in the world.

## 1. The transformation model, horizon by horizon

### 2026 — Measured

**Evidence status:** `measured`, with two near-term modelled figures marked as such. This is the only horizon where the model reports the world rather than reasoning about it. Sources: ILO *Employment and Social Trends 2026* (January 2026), ITU *Facts and Figures 2025*, OECD *Employment Outlook 2025*, IMF exposure analysis, ILO WP140 and the March 2026 gender brief — the most recent evidence in the base (CLAIM-DIV-09).

**What we can say.**

Headline employment is stable. Global unemployment is projected to hold at about 4.9% in 2026, roughly 186 million people (CLAIM-EMP-08); OECD unemployment was 4.9% in May 2025 with an average employment rate of 72.1% and participation at a record high (CLAIM-ECON-11). There is no measured aggregate employment collapse attributable to AI, and no source in the evidence base supplies a measured count of AI-displaced jobs at all.

Stability is not health. The ILO's own framing is that labour markets "remain stable, but this stability is fragile" (CLAIM-SCEN-12), and that the deterioration shows in job quality rather than headline unemployment: nearly 300 million workers in extreme poverty under US$3/day, and 2.1 billion expected in informal jobs by 2026 (CLAIM-EMP-09). Youth carry the sharpest pressure — 12.4% unemployment in 2025, around 260 million NEET, 27.9% NEET in low-income countries — and the ILO explicitly names AI and automation as factors that *could* worsen first-job entry for educated youth in high-income countries (CLAIM-EMP-10). That last part is a warning from the ILO, not a measured AI effect.

Exposure is large, unevenly distributed, and structurally gendered. Almost 40% of global employment is AI-exposed: ~60% advanced economies, 40% emerging markets, 26% low-income countries (CLAIM-EMP-05), splitting in the average advanced economy into 27% high-exposure/high-complementarity and 33% high-exposure/low-complementarity (CLAIM-EMP-06). ILO's refined 2025 index puts one in four workers globally in an occupation with some exposure, 3.3% in the top gradient (CLAIM-AUG-04), rising to 34% of total employment exposed in high-income countries against 11% in low-income countries (CLAIM-DIV-08). Women's employment is 4.7% versus men's 2.4% in the top gradient globally, 9.6% versus 3.5% in high-income countries (CLAIM-DIV-07), and female-dominated occupations are almost twice as likely to be exposed as male-dominated ones — 29% versus 16% (CLAIM-DIV-09). The mechanism is task composition and occupational segregation, not capability (CLAIM-DIV-11).

The precondition for any of this is missing for a quarter of humanity. 2.2 billion people are offline (CLAIM-DIV-01); 94% of people in high-income countries use the internet against 23% in low-income countries, with 96% of the offline population in low- and middle-income countries (CLAIM-DIV-02). Mobile broadband coverage is nearly universal, so the binding constraints are now affordability, quality and skills, not network reach (CLAIM-DIV-05, CLAIM-DIV-06).

Productivity is not booming. ILO's January 2026 flagship states productivity growth is too weak and uneven to drive convergence, with 2026 employment growth diverging by income group — 0.5% upper-middle, 1.8% lower-middle, 3.1% low-income (CLAIM-ECON-09). Measured demographic drag on OECD productivity growth over 2000-2019 was 0.13 percentage points a year (CLAIM-ECON-10).

Algorithmic management is present now, and it does not require AI. It is defined as systems that organise, assign, monitor, supervise and evaluate work, growing across customer service, transport, logistics, banking and healthcare, and definitive of platform work — and rules-based systems count (CLAIM-GOV-09). Over 40% of surveyed freelance platform workers reported regular time-tracking, screenshot submission, and mandated availability (CLAIM-GOV-10). The mechanism of harm is information asymmetry: management sees real-time performance data, workers often do not know the criteria (CLAIM-GOV-11).

Binding law now exists in one jurisdiction. The EU AI Act classifies recruitment, selection, promotion, termination, task allocation and performance monitoring AI as high-risk under Annex III point 4 (CLAIM-GOV-01), requires human oversight with genuine override and stop capability while naming automation bias explicitly (CLAIM-GOV-02), and requires employers to inform workers and their representatives *before* deployment (CLAIM-GOV-03). Non-binding instruments cover more ground: UNESCO's 2021 Recommendation as the first global standard (CLAIM-GOV-05, CLAIM-GOV-06), OECD's Principles with 47 adherents and labour rights named (CLAIM-GOV-07, CLAIM-GOV-08).

**What we cannot say at 2026.** We cannot say how many jobs AI has destroyed or created — nobody measures it. We cannot say what share of employees generally are under surveillance; the 40%+ figure is freelance platform workers only. We cannot attach a percentage to AI hiring bias; the Amazon case is journalism about a decade-old pre-generative system with no published bias magnitude (CLAIM-GOV-12). We cannot say which reskilling programmes work — that gap is documented as the largest in the evidence base. We cannot measure AI literacy in any population. And we cannot claim stable unemployment shows AI has no labour-market effect; it shows no aggregate collapse has been measured.

**Transition drivers into this horizon:** not applicable — this is the baseline.

### 2030 — Projection

**Evidence status:** `projection`. Published forward estimates exist and are cited as such. Two methodologies dominate and they are not equivalent. WEF's figures are extrapolations from a survey of just over 1,000 employers across 55 economies, scaled to a 1.2 billion formal-job base — not a census, not an econometric forecast (CLAIM-EMP-01). BLS runs a formal model but only for the United States, to 2034 (CLAIM-JOB-07). Where they overlap, treat WEF as employer expectation and BLS as national statistical projection; attribute accordingly.

**What we can say.**

The most-cited employer-facing projection is net job *growth*: 170 million created, 92 million displaced, net +78 million, or 7% of today's total employment (CLAIM-EMP-01, CLAIM-EMP-02, CLAIM-EMP-03). The honest reading pairs that with churn — creation plus displacement equals 22% of the formal-job base (CLAIM-EMP-04) — because the same person does not move from the displaced column into the created column.

AI's own share of that is small. Broadening digital access is projected to create 19 million and displace 9 million; AI and information processing 11 million created against 9 million displaced, net +2 million (CLAIM-JOB-05). Attributing the whole 78 million to AI misreads the report: the figures cover technology, demographics, the green transition, geoeconomics and economic pressures in aggregate (CLAIM-EMP-01). And the only macrotrend employers expect to be net-negative for jobs is slower economic growth, not technology (CLAIM-JOB-06 — note the exact digits there need re-checking against the report PDF).

The growth lists split cleanly by measure and this split is the most important corrective in the topic. Fastest-growing by percentage is technology-heavy: Big Data Specialists, FinTech Engineers, AI and Machine Learning Specialists, Software and Applications Developers, with security, IoT, DevOps, UI/UX, environmental and renewable-energy engineers in the top 15 (CLAIM-JOB-01). Largest absolute growth is frontline, care and education: farmworkers, delivery drivers, construction workers, nursing professionals, secondary school teachers — driven by demographics, not primarily AI (CLAIM-JOB-02). BLS reproduces the same pattern with per-occupation numbers: wind turbine service technicians +49.9% and solar PV installers +42.1% lead on percentage while adding fewer than 20,000 jobs combined (CLAIM-JOB-08), whereas home health and personal care aides add +739,800 at median pay of $34,900 against software developers' +267,700 at $133,080 (CLAIM-JOB-09). Growth in jobs is not growth in good jobs.

Decline concentrates in clerical and secretarial work, which tops both the fastest-declining and largest-declining lists — cashiers, administrative assistants and executive secretaries, printing workers, accountants and auditors, postal clerks, bank tellers, data entry clerks — now joined by graphic designers, which WEF attributes explicitly to generative AI reaching creative roles (CLAIM-JOB-03).

Skills churn is high but decelerating. 39% of workers' core skills are expected to be transformed or outdated over 2025-2030, down from 44% in 2023 and 57% in 2020 (CLAIM-SKILL-01). Fastest-growing skills are AI and big data, networks and cybersecurity, technological literacy, alongside rising creative thinking, resilience and curiosity (CLAIM-SKILL-06) — but fastest-growing is not most-important, and analytical thinking remains the top core skill, with a combination of technical and human skills what employers say growing roles need (CLAIM-SKILL-08). Manual dexterity, endurance and precision are the clearest net decliners at 24% (CLAIM-SKILL-07).

The reskilling arithmetic contains its own equity failure. Of 100 workers, 59 need training by 2030: 29 upskilled in role, 19 reskilled and redeployed, and 11 unlikely to receive what they need (CLAIM-SKILL-02) — which WEF itself converts to over 120 million workers at medium-term risk of redundancy (CLAIM-SKILL-03). Employers report 63% naming skill gaps the top barrier to transformation (CLAIM-SKILL-04) and 50% of their workforce having completed training, up from 41% (CLAIM-SKILL-05). Intentions are strong: 85% plan upskilling (CLAIM-RESK-01), 77% plan to reskill workers specifically to work alongside AI, the top AI-response strategy in 45 of 55 economies — while 41% simultaneously expect to downsize as AI capability expands (CLAIM-RESK-09). Both are true of the same employer population and must be presented together.

**What we cannot say at 2030.** These are stated intentions and employer expectations, not outcomes; the gap between the 85% who intend to upskill and delivery is precisely where the 11-in-100 come from. Nothing here tells us whether training works, because no outcome evaluation exists in the evidence base. Informal employment — the majority of global work — is largely outside the WEF base entirely (CLAIM-EMP-09). BLS numbers do not transfer outside the US. And we cannot say any named occupation will or will not exist: "displaced" means the role declines, not that a named individual becomes unemployed (CLAIM-EMP-02).

**Transition drivers from 2026 to 2030.** Employer adoption decisions, which the OECD says determine whether AI helps at all, in a regulatory landscape it describes as containing loopholes and gaps that leave workers vulnerable (CLAIM-ECON-12). Employer-led training delivery converting the 85% intention into completed training (CLAIM-RESK-01, CLAIM-SKILL-05). Demography, which drives the absolute-growth lists in care and education independently of AI (CLAIM-JOB-02) and already subtracts measurably from productivity growth (CLAIM-ECON-10). Macroeconomic conditions, the only net-negative macrotrend in employers' own expectations (CLAIM-JOB-06). Connectivity and affordability, where ITU declines to project convergence (CLAIM-SCEN-11). Phased application of the EU AI Act (CLAIM-GOV-01) and whether comparable rules appear elsewhere — no binding ILO instrument on AI or algorithmic management was verified, so do not assume one (see the source note on S7).

### 2035 — Extrapolation

**Evidence status:** extrapolation. **No published projection reaches this horizon.** BLS stops at 2034 (CLAIM-JOB-07), WEF at 2030, Acemoglu at a ten-year window. Statements here are our reasoning from the observed direction of travel, and carry no source's authority. They are not `projection` in the sense used by the evidence base, and the product must not render them as such (CLAIM-SCEN-13, CLAIM-SCEN-14). The verb register shifts here: "would", "on current direction of travel", not "is projected to".

**What we can say.** Only structural continuations, and only where the direction is corroborated by more than one source.

Task-level reallocation would continue to dominate whole-occupation replacement, because that is what the direction of travel says and because the ILO's revision moved that way rather than the other: hands-on experience *lowered* peak task automation scores from 0.9 to 0.76, with the highest occupational mean at 0.7, meaning even top-gradient occupations retain tasks requiring human input (CLAIM-AUG-06). Exposure would keep broadening beyond clerical work into strongly digitised professional and technical roles, since that expansion is already visible between ILO editions (CLAIM-AUG-08) and already visible in WEF's data via graphic designers (CLAIM-JOB-03). No employment share may be attached to that expansion — the ILO published none (CLAIM-AUG-08).

Reskilling would be continuous rather than a one-time transition. The sectors with the largest training uptake still project the largest remaining need — telecommunications 63%, IT services 62% (CLAIM-RESK-10) — which is the signature of a standing requirement, not a backlog being cleared. Distributional strain would persist and probably compound: training participation collapses with age, one third of 60-65s against over half of 25-44s (CLAIM-SKILL-10), older workers are least able to adapt (CLAIM-ECON-08), and the connectivity gap is not expected to close soon on the statistical agency's own assessment (CLAIM-SCEN-11).

Demography would remain at least as strong a force on labour markets as AI. This follows from magnitude comparison inside the evidence base: measured demographic drag on productivity growth is 0.13pp a year (CLAIM-ECON-10) against Acemoglu's projected AI gain of roughly 0.064pp a year (CLAIM-ECON-01). Demographic drivers also produce the largest absolute job growth in both WEF and BLS (CLAIM-JOB-02, CLAIM-JOB-09), and BLS attributes its deceleration from 13.0% to 3.1% substantially to demographics rather than AI (CLAIM-JOB-07).

**What we cannot say at 2035.** Any number. There is no published figure for this horizon, and constructing one by extending a 2030 projection five more years would fabricate precision the source never claimed. We cannot say which specific occupations will have disappeared or emerged. We cannot say whether AI capability advances, plateaus, or arrives in a form nobody has modelled — the ILO says flatly that this cannot be predicted (CLAIM-SCEN-02), and it warns that technology experts systematically overstate capabilities, with garment production as the documented case where confident automation predictions failed (CLAIM-SCEN-05). The ILO also maintained an explicit "big unknown" category for occupations its framework could not classify and refused to speculate on their direction (CLAIM-SCEN-06); at 2035 much of the occupational map sits in that condition.

**Transition drivers from 2030 to 2035.** Whether AI capability continues improving on hard-to-learn tasks — those with context-dependent decision-making and no objective outcome measure to learn from — which Acemoglu identifies as the binding constraint on further productivity gains and treats with assumptions he calls speculative (CLAIM-ECON-02). Whether task-level productivity findings replicate beyond the single-firm, single-occupation settings that produced them (CLAIM-RESK-02). Whether the 11-in-100 excluded from training in 2030 accumulate into a durable stratum or get reabsorbed (CLAIM-SKILL-02). Whether high-risk-AI regulation spreads beyond the EU, and whether oversight in practice is substantive or the hollow kind EU guidance already describes (CLAIM-GOV-04, CLAIM-ECON-12). Whether connectivity and affordability improve enough for AI-augmented work to be available outside high-income economies (CLAIM-DIV-02, CLAIM-DIV-05).

### 2040 — Scenario

**Evidence status:** `scenario`. Conditional pathways only. Beyond 2035 the model contains no extrapolation either, because two compounding unknowns — technological capability and policy response — make single-track reasoning indefensible. The method here is borrowed, not invented: the ILO's own approach to long horizons is a driver-based scenario set mapping drivers such as innovation, political uncertainty, rule of law and social unrest onto outcomes of job quantity, job quality and income inequality (CLAIM-SCEN-07), and its foresight working paper states that the point is not to anticipate the future as it exactly will be but to prepare for future challenges, treating plausible pathways as roughly equal given the complexity involved (CLAIM-SCEN-08).

**What we can say.** Only conditionals, and only about direction.

If AI capability keeps advancing on hard-to-learn tasks and adoption is broad, then the share of tasks reallocated between humans and machines could be substantially larger than anything currently measured, and exposure could extend well past the professional and technical roles already showing movement (CLAIM-AUG-08). If capability progress concentrates in easy-to-learn tasks instead, aggregate effects could remain close to Acemoglu's upper bounds, which are already modest (CLAIM-ECON-01, CLAIM-ECON-02).

If adoption is broad but the complementarity between AI and high earners is strong, labour income inequality could rise while capital returns raise wealth inequality; if productivity gains are large enough, income levels could instead surge for most workers (CLAIM-ECON-06). Both branches come from the same IMF sentence and neither may be reported without the other. Acemoglu's independent conclusion tightens one side of this: he finds no evidence AI reduces labour income inequality and predicts a widening capital-labour gap, with small real wage declines for some groups (CLAIM-ECON-04) — which contradicts the "AI levels the playing field" reading at the macro level even though task-level evidence shows compression among lower-skilled workers within a firm (CLAIM-RESK-02). That tension is real and should be presented as such.

If the connectivity and preparedness gap persists, the cross-country divide could widen rather than converge: the IMF warns that economies facing less immediate disruption are also less ready to capture AI's advantages, which "could exacerbate the digital divide and cross-country income disparity" (CLAIM-DIV-12), and ITU declines to project closure (CLAIM-SCEN-11). Note the ILO's double-edged reading — lower exposure in poorer countries means less transformation *and* fewer productivity gains (CLAIM-DIV-08).

If the mechanisms behind gendered exposure are left untouched, gendered outcomes could persist in new forms. The ILO states plainly that technologies are not neutral but embedded in societal structures, and warns that past technological waves reproduced gendered divisions in new forms rather than disrupting them (CLAIM-DIV-11), while women remain about 30% of the AI workforce with progress roughly 4 points in six years (CLAIM-DIV-10).

**What we cannot say at 2040.** Anything with a number attached, unless that number is a near-term claim being cited as an anchor for a direction, or is explicitly labelled an illustrative modelling parameter. Nothing about which specific occupations exist. Nothing about relative likelihood between branches, except where the evidence base itself leans — and where it does lean, the lean belongs in the text rather than being hidden behind false balance.

**Transition drivers from 2035 to 2040.** The ILO's own statement is the load-bearing one: outcomes of the technological transition are not predetermined, humans decide to incorporate these technologies and humans must guide the transition (CLAIM-SCEN-04). The concrete decision points: adoption intensity and where it lands in the task distribution; whether governance requires substantive rather than nominal oversight (CLAIM-GOV-02 versus CLAIM-GOV-04); whether reskilling delivery reaches the people the 2030 projections leave out (CLAIM-SKILL-02, CLAIM-SKILL-10); whether infrastructure and affordability constraints ease (CLAIM-DIV-05, CLAIM-DIV-06); and whether new tasks created are socially valuable, since Acemoglu argues formally that some may carry negative social value and that measured GDP growth can therefore overstate welfare (CLAIM-ECON-05).

### 2045 — Scenario, directional only

**Evidence status:** `scenario`. This horizon is roughly two decades past the last credible projection and one full institutional revision cycle past anything measurable — recall that the ILO revised its own task scores materially within two years (CLAIM-AUG-06, CLAIM-AUG-07) and that Acemoglu's headline figures moved between drafts of one paper (CLAIM-ECON-01). Statements at 2045 are therefore restricted to **comparative direction between scenarios**, never to levels.

**What we can say.** That the three pathways in Section 3 are internally coherent, that they differ in specified ways, and that each rests on named conditions traceable to evidence about the *present* direction of travel. That is the whole of the claim. Comparative statements — "higher than", "more concentrated in", "diverging from" — are defensible because they follow from the scenario's own assumptions. Level statements are not.

We can also say what is structurally likely to still be true, because it is slow-moving and multiply corroborated: work will still be organised around tasks rather than whole occupations flipping state (CLAIM-AUG-01, CLAIM-AUG-04); human-contact care work will still be a large employment category, since it is the largest single source of new employment in the strongest available projection and is hard to automate (CLAIM-JOB-09, CLAIM-JOB-02); and distributional outcomes will still be set by policy and deployment choices rather than by technology alone (CLAIM-SCEN-04, CLAIM-ECON-12).

**What we cannot say at 2045.** Whether any named occupation exists. Any figure for employment, productivity, wages, or displacement. Which scenario obtains — only which the current evidence best supports as a direction, which is a different and weaker claim. Whether the technology in use in 2045 resembles what the sources measured at all: every exposure estimate in the base was computed against model generations of 2023-2025, and the Vaccaro meta-analysis covers experiments from January 2020 to June 2023 (CLAIM-RESK-03).

**Transition drivers from 2040 to 2045.** The same drivers as the preceding horizon, compounded, with the addition that by this point path dependency dominates: the cohort excluded from training in the 2030s is at or near end of career by 2045 (CLAIM-SKILL-10), infrastructure built or not built in the 2030s determines who can participate (CLAIM-DIV-02), and governance regimes established early set the oversight baseline for everything after (CLAIM-GOV-01). Decisions taken before 2035 are what make the 2045 scenarios diverge.

## 2. Five transformation dimensions

Each dimension is traced across the horizons in the register appropriate to that horizon. Where a dimension has no evidence at a horizon, that is stated rather than filled in.

### 2.1 Automation — what tasks get done by machines

**The unit is the task, not the job.** This is the most consequential framing decision in the model and it comes from a primary UN source: the ILO finds the most important impact is likely to be augmenting work, automating some tasks within an occupation while leaving time for other duties, as opposed to fully automating occupations (CLAIM-AUG-01).

**2026 (measured/projection).** Concentration is narrow. Only clerical work is highly exposed as a broad occupational group — 24% of clerical tasks highly exposed plus 58% at medium exposure — while for every other occupational group highly exposed tasks run between 1% and 4% and medium-exposed tasks do not exceed 25% (CLAIM-AUG-02). In the refined 2025 index, 3.3% of global employment sits in the highest gradient, and the 13 occupations in gradient 4 are mostly clerical: data entry clerks, typists, accounting and bookkeeping clerks, general office clerks (CLAIM-AUG-04). Automation potential scales with country income — 0.4% of employment in low-income countries against 5.5% in high-income (CLAIM-AUG-03).

Two revisions in the ILO's own estimates move in opposite directions and both must be reported. Peak task-level automation scores fell from 0.9 in 2023 to 0.76 in 2025, with the highest occupational mean at 0.7, because hands-on experience showed the earlier assessment was overly optimistic about full automation (CLAIM-AUG-06). At the same time the share of employment in the top category *rose*, from 2.3% to 3.3%, partly because gradient 4 is defined by mean and dispersion rather than mean alone (CLAIM-AUG-07). Individual tasks look less automatable; slightly more employment sits in the most-exposed bucket.

**2030 (projection).** Displacement of 92 million roles against 170 million created (CLAIM-EMP-02, CLAIM-EMP-01), with AI and information processing specifically accounting for 9 million displaced and 11 million created (CLAIM-JOB-05). The declining lists are clerical and secretarial — the one category topping both the fastest-declining and largest-declining lists — newly joined by graphic designers as generative AI reaches creative work (CLAIM-JOB-03). Robotics and automation is expected to transform business by 58% of employers, below AI and information processing at 86% and broadening digital access at 60% (CLAIM-JOB-04).

**2035 (extrapolation).** Task-level substitution would continue to spread into strongly digitised professional and technical work, which is the direction the ILO's own edition-to-edition revision moved (CLAIM-AUG-08) and which WEF's graphic-designer entry already shows in employer expectations (CLAIM-JOB-03). No share may be attached: the ILO published none for that expansion (CLAIM-AUG-08). Physical-dexterity work would remain comparatively resistant — the garment production case is the documented instance where confident automation predictions failed on pliable materials and stitching dexterity (CLAIM-SCEN-05) — though manual dexterity, endurance and precision are the clearest declining *skills* in employer expectations at 24% (CLAIM-SKILL-07), which is a statement about robotics and process change rather than about generative AI.

**2040-2045 (scenario).** If capability advances on hard-to-learn tasks — those with context-dependent decision-making and no objective outcome measure to learn from, which Acemoglu identifies as the constraint and treats speculatively (CLAIM-ECON-02) — the automatable share could extend well beyond anything currently measured. If it does not, the share could stay close to today's narrow concentration. The evidence base cannot discriminate between these, and the ILO states directly that how generative AI will develop cannot be predicted (CLAIM-SCEN-02) while warning that technology experts systematically overstate capability (CLAIM-SCEN-05). The ILO's "big unknown" — occupations with high mean exposure and high task-level variance that its framework could not classify, and on whose direction it explicitly refused to speculate (CLAIM-SCEN-06) — is the honest status of much of the occupational map at these horizons.

### 2.2 Augmentation — where humans work with AI

**This is the larger category, and the model treats it as the centre of gravity rather than as the optimistic counterpoint.** The ILO's finding is that augmentation potential exceeds automation potential in every income group (CLAIM-AUG-01), and the quantitative gap is wide: augmentation potential affects 10.4% of employment in low-income countries and 13.4% in high-income, with upper-middle at 13.5%, against automation potential of 0.4% and 5.5% respectively (CLAIM-AUG-03). Roughly an order of magnitude larger in low-income countries, and more than double in high-income ones.

**The gradient framing matters as much as the ratio.** The authoritative source moved *away* from a binary automate/augment split and says so explicitly: the 2025 update replaced automation / augmentation / "big unknown" with four progressive gradients, where gradient 1 is low overall exposure with significant task-level variability, aligning closely with the old notion of augmentation, and gradient 4 has the highest share of automation-exposed tasks (CLAIM-AUG-05). Even gradient 4's highest occupational mean of 0.7 means tasks requiring human input remain inside the most-exposed occupations (CLAIM-AUG-06). The ILO also cautions that these classifications are "only illustrative", since task scoring carries subjectivity and capabilities evolve rapidly (CLAIM-AUG-05).

Product consequence: an occupation is not a state, it is a distribution of tasks across gradients. AETHER should render exposure as position on a gradient with visible task-level variance, not as a binary safe/at-risk verdict. The evidence base supports the gradient; it does not support the verdict.

**2026 (measured).** One in four workers globally is in an occupation with some exposure (CLAIM-AUG-04). The IMF's complementarity split is the useful cross-check: in the average advanced economy 27% of employment is high-exposure/high-complementarity — AI likely helps — against 33% high-exposure/low-complementarity, with emerging markets at 16%/24% and low-income countries at 8%/18% (CLAIM-EMP-06). Roughly half of exposed advanced-economy employment falls on the complementarity side. Workers themselves are not expecting upheaval: 26.5% expect no impact and 30.2% only slight impact on jobs in their industry, with complete transformation predicted by few — highest among service and sales workers at 5.1% and clerical support at 4.2% (CLAIM-AUG-09). That survey is Polish, 1,640 respondents, with clerical workers and managers over-represented, and asked about *industry* rather than own job; it is not global worker sentiment.

**2030 (projection).** Augmentation is what employers say they are planning for. Reskilling and upskilling workers to work more effectively *alongside* AI is the most anticipated strategy in 45 of 55 economies, at 77%, with 62% planning to hire people with skills to work with AI (CLAIM-RESK-09). That is an augmentation strategy stated by employers, alongside the 41% who also expect to downsize (CLAIM-RESK-09).

**2035 (extrapolation).** The augmentation share would broaden as exposure spreads into professional and technical roles (CLAIM-AUG-08), and the augmentation/automation ratio would remain above 1 in every income group on current direction of travel (CLAIM-AUG-03), since the ILO's revision moved toward more residual human input rather than less (CLAIM-AUG-06). No figure is available for this horizon.

**2040-2045 (scenario).** If deployment is designed around task-level complementarity, the augmentation share could remain the dominant mode and the productivity gains could be broadly distributed. If deployment optimises for substitution in the low-complementarity third of exposed employment (CLAIM-EMP-06), the same aggregate exposure could produce a very different distributional outcome. Note the ILO's double-edged reading for poorer economies: lower exposure means less disruption *and* fewer productivity gains (CLAIM-DIV-08), and infrastructure constraints — electricity, internet, relative labour cost, digital literacy, finance — are excluded from the exposure models entirely and would "likely increase the productivity gap" (CLAIM-AUG-03).

### 2.3 Human-AI collaboration — the actual teaming

**The central corrective.** The widely asserted claim that human plus AI beats either alone is contradicted by the best available peer-reviewed synthesis. Vaccaro, Almaatouq and Malone's preregistered meta-analysis of 106 experiments and 370 effect sizes found human-AI combinations performed significantly *worse* than the best of human or AI alone: Hedges' g = −0.23, 95% CI −0.39 to −0.07 (CLAIM-RESK-03). This is not smoothed over anywhere in this model, and the product should not smooth it either.

**And its necessary pair.** The same meta-analysis found human-AI systems performed significantly better than humans alone, g = +0.64, 95% CI 0.53 to 0.74, a medium-to-large effect (CLAIM-RESK-04). Both are true and neither may be reported alone: AI makes people better than they were, while the combination still underperforms the stronger single performer. Reporting only the −0.23 is doom-mongering; reporting only the +0.64 is the standard overclaim.

**The moderators are where the actionable content is.**

- *Task type.* Decision tasks — choosing among a finite set of options — show significantly negative synergy at g = −0.27, 95% CI −0.44 to −0.10. Creation tasks — open-response content — show g = +0.19, which is **not individually statistically significant** at n = 34, though the difference between the two task types is significant (CLAIM-RESK-05). The defensible design rule: pair AI with humans for generative work, be far more cautious for discrete decisions. State the significance limitation whenever this is shown.
- *Who is better alone.* When the human outperformed the AI alone, the combination beat both, g = +0.46, 95% CI 0.28 to 0.66. When the AI outperformed the human, the combination lost value relative to AI alone, g = −0.54, 95% CI −0.71 to −0.37 (CLAIM-RESK-06). The authors' explanation: over 95% of studied systems had humans making the final call, and humans who are better overall are also better at judging when to trust the system. Where the AI is stronger, human override destroys value.
- *Explanations and confidence displays did nothing.* Presence of an explanation, inclusion of AI confidence, and participant type were all insignificant moderators across 370+ effect sizes, and the authors suggest de-emphasising that line of inquiry (CLAIM-RESK-07). This is a *performance* finding. It does not undercut the accountability and rights-based case for explainability under OECD and UNESCO principles (CLAIM-GOV-07, CLAIM-GOV-06) — but it does mean explainability should not be sold as a performance fix.
- *The proposed fix is barely tested.* Pre-determined delegation of separate sub-tasks between human and AI — the standard recommendation — appears in only 3 of 100+ experiments, yielding 4 effect sizes at g = 0.22 with a CI of −0.42 to 0.87, crossing zero (CLAIM-RESK-08). This is an evidence gap, not a validated solution. Anyone claiming task-delegation designs are proven is overreaching.

**The genuine friction with governance.** The EU AI Act requires human oversight with the ability to disregard, override or reverse output and to halt the system, and names automation bias explicitly (CLAIM-GOV-02). The performance evidence says human override destroys value precisely where the AI is the stronger performer (CLAIM-RESK-06). These are not reconcilable by assertion. The oversight requirement rests on rights and accountability, not on task performance, and EU guidance itself recognises that formal oversight can be substantively hollow — its worked example describes algorithmic pay-setting where oversight exists formally but pay is determined exclusively by the algorithm and appeals rarely change outcomes (CLAIM-GOV-04). The honest position: oversight is a rights requirement that may carry a measurable performance cost in some task classes, and the evidence base gives no method for resolving that trade-off. AETHER should surface the tension rather than pretend it away.

**Standing limitation across all of the above.** The meta-analysis covers experiments published January 2020 to June 2023, predating current model generations (CLAIM-RESK-03). Its findings may not hold for newer systems. It is nonetheless the strongest evidence available, and no newer synthesis of comparable rigour is in the base.

**2026 (measured).** Field evidence is thin but strong where it exists: generative AI raised customer support productivity 14% in issues resolved per hour on average, 34% for novice and low-skilled workers, with minimal impact on experienced and highly skilled workers, across 5,179 agents, along with improved customer sentiment, increased retention, and fewer requests for managerial intervention (CLAIM-RESK-02). One firm, one occupation, one tool, a pre-2024 model. Do not extrapolate it to the economy — Acemoglu's macro estimate explicitly incorporates task-level findings of this kind and still yields sub-1% aggregate TFP effects (CLAIM-ECON-01).

**2030 (projection).** Employers intend to build teaming capability at scale — 77% reskilling to work alongside AI, 69% recruiting talent skilled in AI tool design (CLAIM-RESK-09) — while the evidence on *how* to team effectively remains, per the meta-analysis, unresolved (CLAIM-RESK-03, CLAIM-RESK-08). That mismatch between intent and method is one of the sharper honest findings in this model.

**2035 (extrapolation).** Collaboration design would be the differentiating variable rather than model capability, because the moderator findings say the same technology produces gains or losses depending on task type and relative competence (CLAIM-RESK-05, CLAIM-RESK-06). This is an inference from the direction of the evidence, not a projection.

**2040-2045 (scenario).** If effective integration processes are found — which the meta-analysis authors name as the open research problem rather than a solved one (CLAIM-RESK-03) — collaborative configurations could outperform both humans and systems alone across more task classes than they do now. If they are not found, deployment could bifurcate: AI-alone for decision tasks where it dominates, human-alone where it does not, with the collaborative middle staying thin. The evidence base supports neither branch over the other.

### 2.4 Skill demand — what shifts

**2026 (measured).** Skill gaps are the top barrier to business transformation, named by 63% of employers (CLAIM-SKILL-04) — with the caveat that employer-reported shortages can be self-serving explanations for hiring difficulty that may have other causes such as pay. Half of the workforce at surveyed employers has completed training, up from 41% in 2023 (CLAIM-SKILL-05), self-reported by large global employers and therefore likely an optimistic upper bound relative to SMEs and informal work. Direction of shift, per OECD synthesis: toward management, business and digital skills, away from some cognitive and clerical tasks (CLAIM-SKILL-09). Note the asymmetry OECD draws in the same passage — exposure is higher for highly educated cognitive workers while automation risk from all technologies is higher for more routine occupations. Exposure and automation risk point at different groups.

Participation is unequal by age: about one third of adults aged 60-65 trained in 2023 against over half of 25-44 (CLAIM-SKILL-10), and the IMF finds older workers potentially less able to adapt (CLAIM-ECON-08). The group most likely to need retraining is least likely to receive it.

**2030 (projection).** 39% of core skills transformed or outdated over 2025-2030, down from 44% and 57% in prior editions (CLAIM-SKILL-01) — the deceleration is as newsworthy as the level, and WEF attributes part of it to more workers having completed training (CLAIM-SKILL-05). Note this is share of *skill sets*, not of jobs. Fastest-growing: AI and big data, networks and cybersecurity, technological literacy, with creative thinking, resilience/flexibility/agility, and curiosity and lifelong learning also rising (CLAIM-SKILL-06). Fastest-growing is not most-important — analytical thinking remains the top core skill and human skills stay critical (CLAIM-SKILL-06, CLAIM-SKILL-08). Clearest decline: manual dexterity, endurance and precision, 24% net (CLAIM-SKILL-07). The requirement employers describe is a combination of technical and human skills, not a substitution of one for the other (CLAIM-SKILL-08) — which makes "learn to code" the wrong reading of this evidence.

**2035 (extrapolation).** Skill demand would be continuous rather than episodic. The sectors with the largest training uptake still project the largest remaining need — telecommunications 63%, IT services 62% (CLAIM-RESK-10) — which is the signature of a standing requirement. The OECD's recommendation follows the same logic: shift to a career model where learning at work takes place throughout life, with expanded career guidance especially for mid-career and older workers (CLAIM-RESK-11). That is a recommendation, not evidence of effectiveness.

**2040-2045 (scenario).** If task composition keeps shifting, the durable skills could be the ones the evidence already marks as complementary rather than substitutable: analytical thinking, creative work, and the judgement needed to know when to trust a system — the last of which the collaboration evidence identifies as the actual differentiator (CLAIM-RESK-06). If capability advances into hard-to-learn tasks (CLAIM-ECON-02), that boundary could move again, and the evidence base gives no basis for saying where it settles. What cannot be said at any horizon: which specific credential or skill will be valuable in 2045. No source supports that, and no measurement of AI literacy exists in any population to anchor it.

### 2.5 Work quality and conditions — algorithmic management, surveillance, oversight, inclusion

This dimension is where the evidence is strongest on mechanism and weakest on prevalence, and where the ILO locates the actual deterioration: job quality, not headline unemployment (CLAIM-EMP-09, CLAIM-SCEN-12).

**2026 (measured).** Algorithmic management is defined as systems using tracked data to organise, assign, monitor, supervise and evaluate work; it is growing across customer service, transport, logistics, banking and healthcare, and is the defining feature of digital labour platforms. Under it, workers interact with a system rather than a human manager, reducing contact with managers and among co-workers. **It does not require AI** — rules-based systems count (CLAIM-GOV-09). Framing it as purely an AI phenomenon overstates the technology's role.

Surveillance is documented for a specific population: over 40% of surveyed freelance platform workers reported regular working-hours tracking, screenshot submission, and required availability at specified times, with monitoring resembling an employment relationship despite self-employed classification (CLAIM-GOV-10). The same source documents self-disciplining behaviour among monitored workers, GPS behavioural tracking of drivers, and pressure from real-time client tracking. **No verified figure exists for surveillance prevalence across the general employed workforce** — do not generalise the platform figure. The mechanism of harm is information asymmetry: management assesses performance on real-time data and complex processing while workers may not know the criteria, results, or implications (CLAIM-GOV-11).

Bias is documented as a mechanism through one canonical case: Amazon built resume-screening models from 2014, found by 2015 they did not rate candidates for technical roles gender-neutrally because the training data was ten years of mostly male resumes, and scrapped the tool (CLAIM-GOV-12). This is journalism based on anonymous sources about a decade-old pre-generative system, useful as illustration of the training-data mechanism, not as evidence of prevalence — and **no quantified AI hiring-bias rate is verifiable**, so no percentage may be attached.

Protection exists but is jurisdictionally partial. Binding: EU AI Act high-risk classification for employment AI (CLAIM-GOV-01), Article 14 oversight with override and stop capability and automation bias named in law (CLAIM-GOV-02), Article 26 pre-deployment notification to workers and their representatives, oversight assigned to persons with competence, training **and authority**, logs kept at least six months, and individuals informed when subject to AI-assisted decisions (CLAIM-GOV-03). Non-binding: UNESCO's 2021 Recommendation requiring investigation and redress of harms, auditability and traceability, transparent self-assessment, and prohibition where use would violate human rights obligations (CLAIM-GOV-06); OECD Principles with human agency and oversight safeguards and labour rights named among risks requiring systematic risk management (CLAIM-GOV-08). No binding ILO instrument on AI or algorithmic management was verified — do not imply one exists comparable in force to the AI Act. OECD states outright that existing regulation contains loopholes and gaps leaving workers vulnerable (CLAIM-ECON-12).

Inclusion baseline: 2.2 billion offline (CLAIM-DIV-01); 94% versus 23% internet use by country income (CLAIM-DIV-02); 77% of men against 71% of women, 85% urban against 58% rural, 82% of 15-24s against 72% of everyone else (CLAIM-DIV-03) — note the urban-rural gap at 27 points is far larger than the 6-point global gender gap. Africa at 36%, LDCs 34%, LLDCs 38% against 88-93% in CIS, Europe and the Americas (CLAIM-DIV-04). Women are 29.2% of STEM workers and about 30% of the AI workforce, roughly 4 points above 2016, with a 5.9% retention decline one year after graduation (CLAIM-DIV-10) — so the same group more exposed to disruption (CLAIM-DIV-09) is underrepresented among those building the systems.

**2030 (projection).** Phased AI Act application extends the notification and oversight baseline across EU-linked deployment (CLAIM-GOV-01). Nothing in the evidence base projects surveillance prevalence, algorithmic management coverage, or job quality to 2030. The ILO's job-quality trajectory is stated for 2026 only, with informality rising (CLAIM-EMP-09). This is a real gap: the product cannot show a job-quality projection because none exists.

**2035 (extrapolation).** On direction of travel, algorithmic management would extend further into standard employment rather than remaining platform-specific, since the ILO already records it growing across five named industries beyond platforms (CLAIM-GOV-09). Whether oversight is substantive or nominal would remain the operative question, given that EU guidance already documents the hollow form (CLAIM-GOV-04). The connectivity gap would still be open: ITU explicitly declines to project convergence despite above-average growth in the least connected groups (CLAIM-SCEN-11).

**2040-2045 (scenario).** If governance requires and enforces substantive oversight — competence, training and authority, not nominal review (CLAIM-GOV-03) — conditions could improve relative to the present platform baseline. If oversight remains formal while decisions are effectively algorithmic (CLAIM-GOV-04), information asymmetry could deepen and extend to a larger share of the workforce. If gendered task composition and occupational segregation are left untouched, outcomes could reproduce in new forms — the ILO's explicit warning, grounded in the observation that technologies are embedded in societal structures rather than neutral, and that past waves reproduced gendered divisions rather than disrupting them (CLAIM-DIV-11), with compounding risk at intersections with race, ethnicity and disability.

## 3. The three 2045 scenarios

**Rules governing this section.** These are conditional pathways, not predictions (CLAIM-SCEN-13). Outcomes are expressed as direction and comparison — higher than, concentrated in, diverging — never as levels. Every number appearing here is either a near-term claim cited as the anchor for a direction, or is explicitly marked as an illustrative modelling parameter carried by no source. The method is the ILO's: drivers mapped onto the outcome triad of job quantity, job quality and income inequality (CLAIM-SCEN-07), with foresight understood as exploring plausible contingent pathways rather than estimating what will happen (CLAIM-SCEN-08).

The three scenarios differ on **two axes**, and the axes are chosen because the evidence base identifies both as unresolved and consequential:

- **Axis A — where deployment lands in the task distribution.** Substitution-weighted, i.e. optimising the low-complementarity third of exposed employment (CLAIM-EMP-06), versus complementarity-weighted, i.e. optimising the augmentation potential that the ILO finds larger in every income group (CLAIM-AUG-01, CLAIM-AUG-03).
- **Axis B — whether governance and reskilling delivery are substantive or nominal.** Real oversight with competence, training and authority (CLAIM-GOV-03) and training that reaches the excluded, versus formal oversight of the hollow kind EU guidance documents (CLAIM-GOV-04) and training that reproduces existing participation gaps (CLAIM-SKILL-10).

Scenario 01 is high on both. Scenario 02 is substitution-weighted with nominal governance. Scenario 03 is complementarity-weighted, with governance quality treated as the open variable — which is what makes it the most fragile of the three rather than simply the nicest.

---

### Scenario 01 — Responsible AI Adoption

**Defining conditions.** Adoption is deliberate and governed. Employment AI is treated as high-risk with enforced oversight requirements, following the only binding template in the evidence base (CLAIM-GOV-01, CLAIM-GOV-02, CLAIM-GOV-03), and comparable regimes exist beyond the EU — which closes the gaps the OECD identifies in current regulation (CLAIM-ECON-12). Oversight is substantive: persons with competence, training and authority, not formal review of decisions the system has already made (CLAIM-GOV-03 versus CLAIM-GOV-04). Reskilling reaches the people current projections exclude, meaning the 11-in-100 who would not receive needed training are materially fewer (CLAIM-SKILL-02, CLAIM-SKILL-03). Connectivity, affordability and skills constraints ease enough for participation outside high-income economies (CLAIM-DIV-02, CLAIM-DIV-05, CLAIM-DIV-06).

**Assumptions this scenario requires and cannot support with evidence.** That reskilling at scale works — no rigorous outcome evaluation exists anywhere in the base, no completion rates, no employment or earnings effects, no cost per successful transition. This is the largest single gap in the evidence base and this scenario depends on it. That enforcement of oversight requirements is effective, which is untested — the AI Act is in phased application and EU guidance already describes the hollow-compliance failure mode (CLAIM-GOV-04). That substantive oversight does not carry an unacceptable performance cost, which sits in tension with the finding that human override destroys value where the AI is the stronger performer (CLAIM-RESK-06).

**Directions of travel.**

- *Productivity:* higher than Scenario 02 on a welfare basis, plausibly lower on measured output. Anchor: Acemoglu's distinction that consumer welfare depends on TFP rather than GDP because additional investment comes out of consumption (CLAIM-ECON-03), plus his formal argument that some AI-created new tasks may carry negative social value so measured GDP can overstate welfare (CLAIM-ECON-05). Governed adoption forgoes some substitution gains and avoids some negative-value task creation.
- *Job transformation:* task-level reallocation dominant, whole-occupation displacement lower than Scenario 02. Anchor: augmentation potential exceeds automation potential in every income group (CLAIM-AUG-01, CLAIM-AUG-03), and this scenario deliberately deploys into that larger share.
- *New occupations:* growth concentrated in governance, oversight and assurance functions, plus the complementary-skill roles the evidence already shows growing. Anchor: the AI Act creates a legal requirement for competent, trained, authorised oversight persons (CLAIM-GOV-03), and UNESCO suggests an independent AI Ethics Officer role and certification mechanisms (CLAIM-GOV-06). This is a real demand mechanism, not a guess — though note UNESCO's own caution that such mechanisms should not disadvantage SMEs and start-ups (CLAIM-GOV-06).
- *Reskilling demand:* highest of the three scenarios, and continuous. Anchor: the most-trained sectors already report the largest remaining need — telecommunications 63%, IT services 62% (CLAIM-RESK-10) — and OECD's conclusion is a career model of lifelong learning rather than a one-time transition (CLAIM-RESK-11).
- *Inequality:* lower than Scenario 02, but **not reduced**. This scenario does not claim AI reduces inequality, because the evidence does not support that: Acemoglu finds no evidence AI reduces labour income inequality and predicts a widening capital-labour gap regardless of distribution across demographic groups (CLAIM-ECON-04). The honest claim is narrower divergence than the alternative, achieved through training reach and oversight, not through the technology.
- *Workplace conditions:* better than Scenario 02 on transparency and recourse. Anchor: pre-deployment notification, override capability, log retention and the right to be informed of AI-assisted decisions (CLAIM-GOV-02, CLAIM-GOV-03), against the documented baseline of information asymmetry (CLAIM-GOV-11) and platform-work monitoring (CLAIM-GOV-10).

**Leading indicators observable before 2035.** High-risk-AI style requirements adopted in jurisdictions beyond the EU. Enforcement actions or documented remediation under Article 26, rather than compliance paperwork alone. Appeal or override rates that actually change outcomes, which is the exact failure the EU pay-setting example describes (CLAIM-GOV-04). Training completion rising among the groups currently excluded — specifically the 60-65 cohort against its one-third baseline (CLAIM-SKILL-10) — rather than rising in aggregate, which can happen while exclusion persists. Publication of the first credible reskilling outcome evaluations, since their absence is what makes this scenario's central assumption unverifiable today. Skill instability continuing to fall past 39% (CLAIM-SKILL-01), which would suggest transformation is being absorbed rather than accumulating.

---

### Scenario 02 — High Automation

**Defining conditions.** Capability advances into hard-to-learn tasks — the ones with context-dependent decision-making and no objective outcome measure to learn from, which Acemoglu identifies as the binding constraint on further gains and treats with assumptions he labels speculative (CLAIM-ECON-02). Adoption is substitution-weighted, concentrating on the 33% of advanced-economy employment that is high-exposure/low-complementarity (CLAIM-EMP-06). Governance remains gapped or nominal (CLAIM-ECON-12, CLAIM-GOV-04). Reskilling stays intention-led rather than delivery-led — the pattern where 77% plan to reskill for AI while 41% simultaneously expect to downsize (CLAIM-RESK-09) resolves toward the second.

**Assumptions this scenario requires and cannot support with evidence.** That AI capability generalises to hard tasks. The evidence points the other way so far: two years of real-world use *lowered* the ILO's peak automation scores from 0.9 to 0.76 (CLAIM-AUG-06), the ILO warns technology experts systematically overstate capability with garment production as the documented failure (CLAIM-SCEN-05), and it states outright that generative AI's development cannot be predicted (CLAIM-SCEN-02). This scenario is not evidence-free — the top-gradient employment share did rise from 2.3% to 3.3% (CLAIM-AUG-07) and exposure did expand into digitised professional roles (CLAIM-AUG-08) — but its central mechanism runs against the direction of the most recent revision.

**Directions of travel.**

- *Productivity:* higher measured output than Scenario 01, with weaker translation into welfare. Anchor: Acemoglu's TFP-versus-GDP distinction and the negative-social-value task argument (CLAIM-ECON-03, CLAIM-ECON-05). Note also that even his upper bounds are modest — 0.66% TFP over ten years, or 0.53% adjusted for hard tasks (CLAIM-ECON-01, CLAIM-ECON-02) — and he calls the 0.66% itself an upper bound on medium-run effects (CLAIM-SCEN-10). A high-automation pathway does not automatically imply a productivity boom, and the current baseline is weak productivity growth, not a surge (CLAIM-ECON-09).
- *Job transformation:* whole-occupation displacement higher than the other two, concentrated where the evidence already concentrates it — clerical and secretarial work, which tops both the fastest-declining and largest-declining lists (CLAIM-JOB-03), the 13 mostly-clerical gradient-4 occupations (CLAIM-AUG-04), and increasingly digitised professional and technical roles (CLAIM-AUG-08). Churn higher than the 22% of formal jobs projected for 2030 (CLAIM-EMP-04). No named occupation is claimed to cease existing.
- *New occupations:* fewer relative to displacement than in Scenario 01 or 03, and more likely to include tasks of contested social value (CLAIM-ECON-05). Note the AI-specific projection anchor is thin even now: AI and information processing nets +2 million to 2030, 11 million created against 9 million displaced (CLAIM-JOB-05).
- *Reskilling demand:* highest in volume, lowest in delivery — the combination that produces the worst outcomes. Anchor: the 11-in-100 who miss out become over 120 million workers at medium-term risk on WEF's own conversion (CLAIM-SKILL-02, CLAIM-SKILL-03); low-skilled workers face more barriers to reskilling and sector-switching, and employer bias treats older workers as less adaptable (CLAIM-RESK-11).
- *Inequality:* diverging most sharply of the three, on three separate mechanisms. Capital-labour gap widening with no relief to labour income inequality (CLAIM-ECON-04). Cross-country divergence, since economies facing less immediate disruption are also least ready to capture the advantages (CLAIM-DIV-12) and the connectivity gap is not expected to close (CLAIM-SCEN-11). Gendered concentration, since female-dominated occupations are almost twice as exposed as male-dominated ones, 29% against 16%, and 9.6% of female against 3.5% of male employment sits in the top gradient in high-income countries (CLAIM-DIV-09, CLAIM-DIV-07). Youth entry pressure, which the ILO already flags as an AI risk for educated first-job seekers in high-income countries (CLAIM-EMP-10).
- *Workplace conditions:* worst of the three. Algorithmic management extending beyond platforms into standard employment (CLAIM-GOV-09) with information asymmetry deepening (CLAIM-GOV-11), monitoring practices of the kind documented for platform workers becoming more widespread (CLAIM-GOV-10 — noting no general-workforce prevalence figure exists to measure this against), and oversight formal rather than effective (CLAIM-GOV-04).

**Leading indicators observable before 2035.** Skill instability reversing upward past 39%, breaking the 57% → 44% → 39% deceleration (CLAIM-SKILL-01). Occupational categories beyond clerical entering the fastest-declining lists — the graphic designer entry is the first instance of this and is explicitly attributed to generative AI (CLAIM-JOB-03), so a second and third such entry in the next WEF edition would be the signal. ILO's next exposure revision moving peak task scores back *up* toward or past 0.9, reversing CLAIM-AUG-06. Gradient-4 employment share rising materially above 3.3% (CLAIM-AUG-04, CLAIM-AUG-07). Employer downsizing intentions rising above the 41% baseline while reskilling intentions fall from 77% (CLAIM-RESK-09). Headline unemployment breaking from the stable ~4.9% band (CLAIM-EMP-08) — the least sensitive indicator, and the last to move, since job quality deteriorates before headline unemployment does (CLAIM-EMP-09, CLAIM-SCEN-12).

### Scenario 03 — Human-AI Collaboration

**Defining conditions.** Work is reorganised around task-level complementarity: deployment targets the augmentation share the ILO finds larger in every income group (CLAIM-AUG-01, CLAIM-AUG-03) and the high-complementarity 27% of advanced-economy employment (CLAIM-EMP-06). Critically, this scenario also requires something the evidence base does *not* yet contain — effective processes for integrating humans and AI, which the Vaccaro authors name as the open research problem (CLAIM-RESK-03). Governance quality is the open variable here rather than an assumption, which is why this scenario can deliver either broadly shared gains or a well-designed version of Scenario 02's distribution.

**Assumptions this scenario requires and cannot support with evidence.** That the human-AI synergy problem gets solved. Right now the pooled effect for synergy is negative at g = −0.23 (CLAIM-RESK-03); the augmentation effect over humans alone is real and sizeable at g = +0.64 (CLAIM-RESK-04), but the combination still underperforms the better single performer. Gains concentrate in creation tasks at g = +0.19, which is not individually significant (CLAIM-RESK-05), and the most-proposed fix — pre-determined task delegation — has 3 studies, 4 effect sizes and a CI crossing zero (CLAIM-RESK-08). This scenario's core mechanism is currently an evidence gap, not a validated capability. Saying so is the point.

**Directions of travel.**

- *Productivity:* gains concentrated in creation and generative work rather than decision work, following the only significant task-type moderator available (CLAIM-RESK-05). Distribution skewed toward less experienced workers within occupations — the strongest field anchor is +14% average and +34% for novices with minimal effect on the experienced (CLAIM-RESK-02), one firm, one occupation, one tool, pre-2024 model. Aggregate effect still plausibly modest, since Acemoglu's macro estimate already incorporates task-level findings of exactly this kind and yields sub-1% TFP over ten years (CLAIM-ECON-01).
- *Job transformation:* highest rate of *role redefinition* with the lowest rate of *role elimination* among the three. Anchor: transformation of jobs is the most likely impact because most occupations consist of tasks requiring human input (CLAIM-AUG-04), and even gradient-4 occupations retain such tasks at a 0.7 occupational mean (CLAIM-AUG-06).
- *New occupations:* concentrated in collaboration design, workflow integration and interaction design — roles that follow from the moderator findings, since which tasks go to whom and who holds final judgement is what determines whether the combination gains or loses (CLAIM-RESK-05, CLAIM-RESK-06). Evidence status of these roles is EMERGING, not GROWING; see Section 5.
- *Reskilling demand:* high and qualitatively different — weighted toward judgement about when to rely on a system rather than toward tool operation. Anchor: humans who outperform the AI are also better at judging when to trust it, which is the mechanism behind the +0.46/−0.54 split (CLAIM-RESK-06). Skill demand pattern matches what employers already describe: technical and human skills together, not one substituting for the other (CLAIM-SKILL-08, CLAIM-SKILL-06).
- *Inequality:* the genuinely contested outcome, and the model should not resolve it. Within-firm compression is evidenced — novices gain most (CLAIM-RESK-02). Macro-level compression is not: Acemoglu shows theoretically that AI improving low-skill productivity in certain tasks may *increase* rather than reduce inequality, finds no evidence of labour income inequality relief, and predicts small real wage declines for some groups including low-education white native-born women (CLAIM-ECON-04). The IMF's conditional runs both ways in one sentence: inequality may rise if complementarity with high earners is strong, or incomes could surge for most workers if productivity gains are large enough (CLAIM-ECON-06). Anyone claiming collaboration is inherently equalising is contradicting the macro evidence.
- *Workplace conditions:* better than Scenario 02 if governance is substantive; not automatically better otherwise. Collaboration-centred deployment is fully compatible with algorithmic management and monitoring — the ILO notes algorithmic management does not even require AI (CLAIM-GOV-09) — so this scenario's conditions outcome depends on Axis B, not on the collaboration design itself.

**Leading indicators observable before 2035.** Replication of human-AI synergy findings with post-2023 models — the single most informative indicator available, since the current meta-analysis covers January 2020 to June 2023 (CLAIM-RESK-03). A larger body of pre-determined-delegation studies moving g = 0.22 to a CI that excludes zero (CLAIM-RESK-08). Productivity findings like the +14%/+34% result replicating outside single-firm settings (CLAIM-RESK-02). Employer reskilling intent at 77% converting into measured completion among workers who work alongside AI (CLAIM-RESK-09, CLAIM-SKILL-05). Interaction-design and collaboration-design roles appearing by name in a WEF or BLS ranked list, which they currently do not (CLAIM-JOB-01, CLAIM-JOB-08). Whether the decision-task penalty persists (CLAIM-RESK-05) — if it does, collaborative deployment stays confined to generative work and this scenario's reach is narrower than its name implies.

---

### Which scenario the evidence best supports

**Scenario 03, with Scenario 01's governance conditions as the determinant of whether its outcomes are good.** Stating that plainly rather than treating the three as equally likely, because the evidence base does lean, and false balance would be its own form of dishonesty.

The reasons, in order of strength:

1. **Augmentation exceeds automation in every income group, from a primary UN source** (CLAIM-AUG-01), by roughly 10.4% against 0.4% in low-income countries and 13.4% against 5.5% in high-income (CLAIM-AUG-03). This is the most direct evidence for the collaboration pathway being the larger phenomenon and against high automation being the modal outcome.
2. **The authoritative source revised toward more residual human input, not less.** Peak task scores fell from 0.9 to 0.76 after two years of real-world use, and the highest occupational mean is 0.7 — tasks requiring humans persist inside the most exposed occupations (CLAIM-AUG-06). The revision that matters most went against Scenario 02.
3. **Transformation, not replacement, is the source's own stated most-likely impact**, because most occupations consist of tasks requiring human input (CLAIM-AUG-04).
4. **Automation exposure is narrow where it is measured.** Only clerical work is highly exposed as a broad group; everything else runs 1-4% highly exposed (CLAIM-AUG-02), and 3.3% of global employment sits in the top gradient (CLAIM-AUG-04).
5. **Employers describe an augmentation strategy, not a replacement strategy**, as their most anticipated response in 45 of 55 economies (CLAIM-RESK-09) — though these are intentions, and 41% expect to downsize too.
6. **No aggregate employment collapse has been measured**, with headline unemployment stable at ~4.9% (CLAIM-EMP-08) and no verified count of AI-displaced jobs anywhere.
7. **Experts systematically overstate automation capability**, documented with a case where it happened (CLAIM-SCEN-05), and reinforced by the ILO revising its own scores down (CLAIM-AUG-06).

**The countervailing evidence, stated rather than buried.** Scenario 02 is not a straw man. Top-gradient employment share rose from 2.3% to 3.3% between ILO editions (CLAIM-AUG-07). Exposure expanded into strongly digitised professional and technical roles (CLAIM-AUG-08). Graphic designers entered the fastest-declining list with WEF attributing it explicitly to generative AI (CLAIM-JOB-03). Clerical and secretarial work tops both decline lists simultaneously (CLAIM-JOB-03). And every exposure estimate in the base was computed against 2023-2025 model generations, so the capability premise of Scenario 02 is unfalsified rather than refuted.

**And the honest limit on this ranking.** Support for Scenario 03 as the *direction* of transformation says nothing about whether its outcomes are good. Its core mechanism — effective human-AI integration — is currently an evidence gap with a negative pooled synergy effect (CLAIM-RESK-03) and a barely-tested proposed fix (CLAIM-RESK-08). Its distributional outcome is contested at the macro level even where task-level compression is measured (CLAIM-ECON-04 against CLAIM-RESK-02). Its conditions outcome depends entirely on governance, since collaboration-centred work is fully compatible with algorithmic management (CLAIM-GOV-09). Scenario 01 is the pathway with the best outcomes and the weakest evidentiary support for its central assumption, because nobody has shown that reskilling at scale works. Scenario 03 is where the direction of travel points; Scenario 01 is what would have to be added for that direction to be worth arriving at. Neither is a prediction — the ILO's own statement is that the outcomes of the transition are not predetermined, that humans decide to incorporate these technologies, and that humans need to guide the transition (CLAIM-SCEN-04).

## 4. The five questions

### Q1. What is happening to work?

**Answer.** Tasks are being reallocated inside occupations faster than occupations are being eliminated. Around one in four workers globally is in an occupation with some generative-AI exposure and 3.3% sit in the highest exposure gradient (CLAIM-AUG-04); the IMF puts total exposure near 40% globally, 60% in advanced economies, 26% in low-income countries (CLAIM-EMP-05). Exposure concentrates in clerical work — 24% of clerical tasks highly exposed against 1-4% for other broad groups (CLAIM-AUG-02) — and is now extending into strongly digitised professional and technical roles (CLAIM-AUG-08) and into creative work, with graphic designers newly on WEF's declining list (CLAIM-JOB-03).

Aggregate employment has not collapsed. Unemployment is projected stable near 4.9% in 2026 (CLAIM-EMP-08), OECD employment and participation are at record highs (CLAIM-ECON-11), and the most-cited employer projection for 2030 is net growth of 78 million jobs (CLAIM-EMP-03). The disruption shows up as churn — 22% of formal jobs (CLAIM-EMP-04) — and in job quality: nearly 300 million workers in extreme poverty, 2.1 billion in informal work (CLAIM-EMP-09), with the ILO calling the stability itself fragile (CLAIM-SCEN-12). Alongside this, algorithmic management is spreading beyond platforms into customer service, transport, logistics, banking and healthcare, and does not require AI to operate (CLAIM-GOV-09).

**Limits.** No source measures jobs actually lost to AI — every displacement figure in the base is a projection or an exposure estimate. Exposure is not job loss, and the ~40% and one-in-four figures are the most commonly misread numbers in this field (CLAIM-EMP-05, CLAIM-SCEN-03). WEF's figures are extrapolations from just over 1,000 employers across 55 economies scaled to 1.2 billion formal jobs, largely excluding the informal majority (CLAIM-EMP-01, CLAIM-EMP-09). Beyond 2030 there is no projection at all, and no named occupation can be said to be ending.

### Q2. What skills will matter?

**Answer.** Technical and human skills together, not one instead of the other (CLAIM-SKILL-08). Fastest-growing to 2030: AI and big data, networks and cybersecurity, technological literacy, with creative thinking, resilience/flexibility/agility and curiosity/lifelong learning also rising (CLAIM-SKILL-06). Fastest-growing is not most-important — analytical thinking remains the top core skill (CLAIM-SKILL-06). Demand is shifting toward management, business and digital skills and away from some cognitive and clerical tasks (CLAIM-SKILL-09). The clearest decline is manual dexterity, endurance and precision at 24% net (CLAIM-SKILL-07). 39% of core skill sets are expected to be transformed or outdated by 2030 — down from 44% in 2023 and 57% in 2020, and that deceleration is part of the finding (CLAIM-SKILL-01).

One skill the collaboration evidence singles out: judgement about when to rely on a system. Humans who outperform the AI are also better at knowing when to trust it, which is the mechanism behind synergy at g = +0.46 where humans are stronger and g = −0.54 where the AI is (CLAIM-RESK-06).

**Limits.** "39%" is share of skill sets, not of jobs (CLAIM-SKILL-01). Skill lists come from employer expectations to 2030 and nothing extends further. No measurement of AI literacy exists in any population, so nothing supports a claim about population-level skill attainment. And no specific credential or skill can be named as valuable in 2045 — the boundary between complemented and substituted tasks moves with capability, which cannot be predicted (CLAIM-SCEN-02).

### Q3. What careers could emerge or grow?

**Answer.** Two different lists, and conflating them is the standard error. By percentage growth, technology roles lead: Big Data Specialists, FinTech Engineers, AI and Machine Learning Specialists, Software and Applications Developers, plus security, IoT, DevOps, UI/UX and renewable-energy roles (CLAIM-JOB-01); BLS puts wind turbine service technicians at +49.9% and solar PV installers at +42.1% (CLAIM-JOB-08). By absolute growth, frontline, care and education roles lead: farmworkers, delivery drivers, construction workers, nursing professionals, secondary school teachers (CLAIM-JOB-02), and in BLS terms home health and personal care aides at +739,800 against software developers at +267,700 (CLAIM-JOB-09). BLS names computer and mathematical occupations the second fastest-growing group at +10.1%, attributing part of it to demand for AI development — AI as job creator in an official projection — while healthcare support grows faster still at +12.4% (CLAIM-JOB-10).

The pay contrast belongs with the growth figures: the largest-growth US occupation pays a median $34,900 while the second-largest pays $133,080 (CLAIM-JOB-09). Growth in jobs is not growth in good jobs.

**Limits.** The two fastest-growing BLS occupations add fewer than 20,000 jobs combined — BLS supplies that caveat itself (CLAIM-JOB-08). BLS is US-only; WEF is employer expectation to 2030. Neither list reaches 2035, let alone 2045. Most of the career titles a 2045 product wants to show do not appear in any ranked list, which is handled explicitly in Section 5.

### Q4. How can people prepare safely and inclusively?

**Answer, and this is the question where the evidence is weakest.** The need is firmly established: 59 of 100 workers need training by 2030, 29 upskilled in role, 19 reskilled and redeployed, and 11 unlikely to receive it (CLAIM-SKILL-02) — over 120 million at medium-term risk on WEF's own conversion (CLAIM-SKILL-03). Employer intent is high: 85% plan upskilling (CLAIM-RESK-01), 77% plan reskilling specifically for working with AI (CLAIM-RESK-09), and completion has risen from 41% to 50% (CLAIM-SKILL-05). Reskilling is continuous, not a one-time transition — the most-trained sectors project the largest remaining need (CLAIM-RESK-10), and OECD's conclusion is a career model of lifelong learning with expanded guidance for mid-career and older workers (CLAIM-RESK-11).

Inclusion has a floor below skills. 2.2 billion people are offline (CLAIM-DIV-01); internet use runs 94% in high-income against 23% in low-income countries (CLAIM-DIV-02), 85% urban against 58% rural (CLAIM-DIV-03), 36% in Africa (CLAIM-DIV-04). Training participation collapses with age — one third of 60-65s against over half of 25-44s (CLAIM-SKILL-10) — and older workers are least able to adapt (CLAIM-ECON-08). Women are more exposed (CLAIM-DIV-09, CLAIM-DIV-07) and underrepresented among those building the systems at about 30% of the AI workforce (CLAIM-DIV-10).

Safety has one binding instrument. Employment AI is high-risk under the EU AI Act (CLAIM-GOV-01), with oversight that must include genuine override and stop capability and awareness of automation bias (CLAIM-GOV-02), pre-deployment notification to workers and their representatives, oversight persons with competence, training and authority, and the right to be informed of AI-assisted decisions (CLAIM-GOV-03). UNESCO adds redress, auditability and prohibition where use would violate human rights obligations (CLAIM-GOV-06); OECD names labour rights among risks requiring systematic management (CLAIM-GOV-08). Both are non-binding.

**Limits, stated plainly.** **No reskilling approach can be called proven effective.** There is no outcome evaluation in the evidence base — no completion rates against employment or earnings effects, no cost per successful transition (documented as the single largest gap). The need is well evidenced; the solutions are not. Employer figures are stated intentions, and the gap between the 85% who intend and delivery is where the 11-in-100 come from (CLAIM-RESK-01, CLAIM-SKILL-02). Binding protection covers EU-linked deployment only, and OECD says existing regulation contains loopholes and gaps leaving workers vulnerable (CLAIM-ECON-12). No binding ILO instrument on AI or algorithmic management was verified. Formal oversight can be hollow — EU guidance documents a case where oversight existed on paper while the algorithm decided and appeals rarely changed outcomes (CLAIM-GOV-04). No quantified AI hiring-bias rate exists (CLAIM-GOV-12), and no surveillance-prevalence figure exists for the general workforce (CLAIM-GOV-10).

### Q5. What could different AI adoption scenarios mean for the economy?

**Answer.** Direction depends on deployment and policy, not on the technology. The evidence base contains genuine, unresolved disagreement and the product should present it rather than resolve it. Acemoglu's peer-reviewed estimate puts AI's ten-year TFP gain at no more than 0.66%, or under 0.53% once hard-to-learn tasks are accounted for, with GDP at 0.93-1.16% rising to ~1.4-1.56% under stronger investment response (CLAIM-ECON-01, CLAIM-ECON-02, CLAIM-ECON-03) — and he calls even that an upper bound built partly on assumptions he labels speculative (CLAIM-SCEN-10). Firm-level field evidence finds +14% average and +34% novice productivity gains in a single occupation (CLAIM-RESK-02). Both are credible; they measure different things — economy-wide aggregate versus task-level.

For scale: measured demographic drag on OECD productivity growth is 0.13pp a year (CLAIM-ECON-10) against Acemoglu's projected AI gain of roughly 0.064pp a year (CLAIM-ECON-01). Demography is not the smaller force in these numbers. Current productivity growth is weak and uneven, not surging (CLAIM-ECON-09).

On distribution, the honest reading is that AI is not expected to reduce inequality on its own. Acemoglu finds no evidence of labour income inequality relief and predicts a widening capital-labour gap, with small real wage declines for some groups (CLAIM-ECON-04), and argues some new AI-created tasks may carry negative social value so measured GDP can overstate welfare (CLAIM-ECON-05). The IMF's conditional runs both ways in a single sentence: inequality may rise if complementarity with high earners is strong, or incomes could surge for most workers if productivity gains are large enough (CLAIM-ECON-06). Cross-country divergence is the more consistent warning: less-disrupted economies are also less ready to capture the benefits, which could exacerbate the digital divide and income disparity (CLAIM-DIV-12), and ITU declines to project convergence (CLAIM-SCEN-11).

Scenario-level directions are in Section 3, expressed comparatively only.

**Limits.** The IMF states directly that AI's exact economic implications are challenging to predict, with uncertainty reminiscent of past general-purpose technologies such as electricity (CLAIM-ECON-07). Acemoglu's figures exist in two live versions — 0.71%/0.55% in the 2024 working paper, 0.66%/0.53% in the 2025 published version — and must not be mixed in one chart (CLAIM-ECON-01). The 14% result is one firm, one occupation, one tool, a pre-2024 model (CLAIM-RESK-02). Consultancy figures widely circulated in this space were deliberately not used. And no scenario in this model carries a 2045 economic figure, because none can be sourced.

## 5. Emerging and growing career categories

**Three labels, used strictly.**

| Label | Meaning | Requirement |
|---|---|---|
| **GROWING** | Evidenced growth in a published projection | Must cite a CLAIM- id naming the role or its occupational group |
| **EMERGING** | Plausible from direction of travel, absent from every ranked list | Must name the nearest evidenced adjacent role |
| **SCENARIO** | Meaningful only under a particular 2045 pathway | Must name the scenario and its conditions |

**The two-list rule.** Percentage growth and absolute growth produce almost entirely different lists and each carries a different meaning. WEF's fastest-growing is technology-heavy (CLAIM-JOB-01); WEF's largest absolute growth is frontline, care and education (CLAIM-JOB-02). BLS Table 1.3 and Table 1.4 split the same way (CLAIM-JOB-08, CLAIM-JOB-09). A card showing only percentage growth for a small-base occupation misleads even when correctly cited — BLS's own caveat is that its two fastest-growing occupations add fewer than 20,000 jobs combined (CLAIM-JOB-08). Every career card should carry which list it comes from.

### GROWING — named in a published projection

Fastest growing by percentage:

- **Big Data Specialists** — WEF's top fastest-growing role (CLAIM-JOB-01).
- **AI and Machine Learning Specialists** — WEF top four (CLAIM-JOB-01).
- **FinTech Engineers** — WEF top four (CLAIM-JOB-01).
- **Software and Applications Developers** — WEF top four (CLAIM-JOB-01); BLS software developers +267,700, second-largest absolute growth, median $133,080 (CLAIM-JOB-09). One of very few roles on both lists.
- **Data Scientists / Data Analysts** — WEF top 15 (CLAIM-JOB-01); BLS +33.5%, 245,900 → 328,300 (CLAIM-JOB-08).
- **Information Security Analysts / Security Management Specialists** — WEF top 15 (CLAIM-JOB-01); BLS +29% (CLAIM-JOB-08).
- **DevOps Engineers, Data Warehousing Specialists, IoT Specialists, UI and UX Designers** — WEF top 15 (CLAIM-JOB-01).
- **Wind Turbine Service Technicians** — BLS fastest-growing occupation at +49.9%, 13,600 → 20,500 (CLAIM-JOB-08). Show with the small-base caveat.
- **Solar Photovoltaic Installers** — BLS +42.1%, 28,600 → 40,600 (CLAIM-JOB-08). Same caveat.
- **Renewable Energy Engineers, Environmental Engineers, Autonomous and Electric Vehicle Specialists** — WEF top 15 (CLAIM-JOB-01).
- **Nurse Practitioners** — BLS +40.1%, 320,400 → 448,800 (CLAIM-JOB-08).

Largest absolute growth:

- **Home Health and Personal Care Aides** — BLS +739,800, the largest single source of new US employment, median pay $34,900 (CLAIM-JOB-09). Show the pay figure; growth is not quality.
- **Registered Nurses** (+166,100), **General and Operations Managers** (+164,000), **Stockers and Order Fillers** (+235,000), **Fast Food and Counter Workers** (+233,200), **Cooks, Restaurant** (+217,000) — BLS Table 1.4 (CLAIM-JOB-09).
- **Nursing Professionals, Secondary School Teachers, Farmworkers, Delivery Drivers, Construction Workers** — WEF largest absolute growth, driven by demographics rather than AI (CLAIM-JOB-02). Note delivery drivers also appear in WEF's fastest-growing top 10 (CLAIM-JOB-01).

Occupational-group level:

- **Computer and Mathematical occupations** — BLS +10.1%, second fastest-growing group, growth attributed partly to demand for continued AI development (CLAIM-JOB-10). This is the strongest official evidence of AI as a job creator in the base.
- **Healthcare Support** (+12.4%), **Healthcare Practitioners and Technical** (+7.2%), **Community and Social Service** (+6.6%) — BLS (CLAIM-JOB-10).

### EMERGING — direction of travel, no ranked-list evidence

Each of these is a reasoned inference. None appears by name in WEF's or BLS's ranked lists, and the product must not display them with a projection basis.

- **AI oversight / assurance roles.** Nearest evidenced adjacent: Information Security Analysts (CLAIM-JOB-08) and Security Management Specialists (CLAIM-JOB-01). Mechanism, which is genuinely strong: the AI Act requires oversight assigned to persons with competence, training and authority (CLAIM-GOV-03), and UNESCO suggests an independent AI Ethics Officer role and certification mechanisms (CLAIM-GOV-06). A legal requirement is a demand mechanism, but no projection counts these jobs.
- **Human-AI workflow / collaboration design.** Nearest evidenced adjacent: UI and UX Designers (CLAIM-JOB-01). Mechanism: the moderator findings make task allocation and final-judgement design the variable that determines whether teaming gains or loses (CLAIM-RESK-05, CLAIM-RESK-06). Note the tension — the most-proposed version of this work, pre-determined task delegation, has 3 studies and a CI crossing zero (CLAIM-RESK-08). The role is emerging; its effectiveness is unproven.
- **Care roles with AI-augmented workflows.** Nearest evidenced adjacent: Home Health and Personal Care Aides (CLAIM-JOB-09), Nursing Professionals (CLAIM-JOB-02), Nurse Practitioners (CLAIM-JOB-08). Mechanism: the largest employment growth is in human-contact work that is hard to automate, so the plausible change is workflow rather than headcount. The pay problem travels with it (CLAIM-JOB-09).
- **Algorithmic management auditing / worker-data roles.** Nearest evidenced adjacent: Information Security Analysts (CLAIM-JOB-08). Mechanism: information asymmetry between management and workers (CLAIM-GOV-11) plus auditability and traceability obligations (CLAIM-GOV-06) and Article 26 log retention (CLAIM-GOV-03).
- **Reskilling and career-transition specialists.** Nearest evidenced adjacent: Community and Social Service occupations, BLS +6.6% (CLAIM-JOB-10). Mechanism: 59 in 100 needing training with 11 likely excluded (CLAIM-SKILL-02) and OECD calling for expanded career guidance (CLAIM-RESK-11). Honest caveat: nobody has shown these interventions work.

### SCENARIO — meaningful only under a specific 2045 pathway

- **Large-scale AI governance and compliance functions** — Scenario 01, conditional on high-risk-style requirements spreading beyond the EU and being enforced substantively rather than nominally (CLAIM-GOV-01, CLAIM-GOV-04, CLAIM-ECON-12).
- **Formalised human-AI teaming specialists** — Scenario 03, conditional on the synergy problem being solved, which is currently an open research question with a negative pooled effect (CLAIM-RESK-03, CLAIM-RESK-08).
- **Large-scale transition and displacement support functions** — Scenario 02, conditional on displacement exceeding reskilling delivery, the pathway in which the 11-in-100 become a durable stratum (CLAIM-SKILL-02, CLAIM-SKILL-03).

### The six careers the brief names

Assessed honestly. Four of the six have only indirect support.

**AI Product Designer — EMERGING.** No ranked list contains it. Nearest evidenced adjacent: **UI and UX Designers**, in WEF's fastest-growing top 15 (CLAIM-JOB-01), and Software and Applications Developers (CLAIM-JOB-01, CLAIM-JOB-09). Support is real but indirect: design skills are growing and AI and big data is the fastest-growing skill (CLAIM-SKILL-06), so the combination is plausible. The countervailing evidence must be shown too — graphic designers are newly on WEF's *declining* list with generative AI named as the cause (CLAIM-JOB-03), so design is not uniformly a growth area. Do not present this as GROWING.

**Human-AI Interaction Designer — EMERGING.** Not in any ranked list. Nearest evidenced adjacent: **UI and UX Designers** (CLAIM-JOB-01). This has the strongest *mechanistic* case of the six, because the collaboration evidence identifies interaction design choices as decisive: task type determines whether synergy is positive or negative (CLAIM-RESK-05), and relative competence determines whether human final-say helps or destroys value (CLAIM-RESK-06). Note the deflating finding — explanations and AI confidence displays, the standard interaction-design levers, were insignificant moderators of performance across 370+ effect sizes (CLAIM-RESK-07). The role is well motivated; its established toolkit is not evidenced to work.

**AI Safety Specialist — EMERGING, with the strongest regulatory anchor of the six.** Not in any ranked list, so not GROWING. But the demand mechanism is binding law rather than speculation: high-risk classification for employment AI (CLAIM-GOV-01), Article 14 oversight requirements naming automation bias (CLAIM-GOV-02), and Article 26 requiring oversight persons with competence, training and authority (CLAIM-GOV-03). UNESCO adds auditability, redress and a suggested AI Ethics Officer role (CLAIM-GOV-06); OECD requires systematic risk management including labour rights (CLAIM-GOV-08). Nearest evidenced adjacent: **Information Security Analysts** at BLS +29% (CLAIM-JOB-08) and Security Management Specialists (CLAIM-JOB-01). Caveat: the binding requirement covers EU-linked deployment, and UNESCO cautions that certification mechanisms should not disadvantage SMEs and start-ups (CLAIM-GOV-06).

**Robotics Systems Engineer — EMERGING, adjacent to GROWING.** Robotics does not appear as a named role in WEF's fastest-growing list. What is evidenced: 58% of employers expect robotics and automation to transform their business by 2030 (CLAIM-JOB-04), and **Autonomous and Electric Vehicle Specialists** are in WEF's fastest-growing top 15 (CLAIM-JOB-01) — the nearest evidenced adjacent role, along with Computer and Mathematical occupations at BLS +10.1% (CLAIM-JOB-10). Countervailing evidence worth showing: physical dexterity has repeatedly resisted automation, with the ILO's garment-production case as the documented failure of confident predictions (CLAIM-SCEN-05).

**AI Governance Specialist — EMERGING.** Overlaps substantially with AI Safety Specialist and rests on the same anchors: Annex III classification (CLAIM-GOV-01), Article 26 deployer obligations including pre-deployment worker notification (CLAIM-GOV-03), UNESCO's redress and auditability requirements (CLAIM-GOV-06), OECD's accountability principle naming labour rights (CLAIM-GOV-08). Nearest evidenced adjacent: Security Management Specialists (CLAIM-JOB-01) and Community and Social Service occupations (CLAIM-JOB-10) — a weak adjacency, which should be said. No projection counts governance roles. The honest framing: a legally mandated function without an occupational projection behind it. If the product shows both this and AI Safety Specialist, distinguish them or merge them; the evidence does not separate them.

**Climate Technology Specialist — GROWING, and the best-evidenced of the six.** This is the one with direct projection support in both sources. WEF's fastest-growing top 15 includes **Renewable Energy Engineers**, **Environmental Engineers** and **Autonomous and Electric Vehicle Specialists** (CLAIM-JOB-01), and 41% of employers expect energy generation, storage and distribution advances to transform their business (CLAIM-JOB-04). BLS's two fastest-growing occupations are **wind turbine service technicians** at +49.9% and **solar photovoltaic installers** at +42.1% (CLAIM-JOB-08). Mandatory caveat, supplied by BLS itself: those two combined add fewer than 20,000 jobs (CLAIM-JOB-08). High percentage growth from a small base. Show the absolute numbers next to the percentages or the card misleads.

**Summary of the six.** One GROWING (Climate Technology Specialist), five EMERGING, none SCENARIO-only. Two rest on binding legal requirements (AI Safety, AI Governance) which is a stronger mechanism than most emerging-role claims but still not a projection. Two rest on UI/UX adjacency (AI Product Designer, Human-AI Interaction Designer), one of which faces countervailing decline evidence in its parent field (CLAIM-JOB-03). One rests on employer transformation expectations and an adjacent specialist role (Robotics Systems Engineer).

## 6. What the product cannot show

Recorded here so the build does not have to rediscover it.

- **No 2045 figures of any kind.** Nothing in the evidence base reaches past 2034 (CLAIM-SCEN-01). Any 2045 number would be fabricated.
- **No job-quality, surveillance, or algorithmic-management projection at any horizon.** These are measured at 2026 and nowhere else. There is no projection to extend.
- **No reskilling effectiveness claim.** The largest gap in the base. Need is evidenced; outcomes are not.
- **No quantified AI hiring-bias rate,** no general-workforce surveillance prevalence, no AI literacy measurement, no employer training spend in currency terms, and no measured count of AI-displaced jobs.
- **No verdict on any named occupation's survival** at any horizon.
- **Three figures need re-checking before charting:** WEF's slower-growth job digits (CLAIM-JOB-06), OECD's 74.6% participation rate (CLAIM-ECON-11), and the ITU affordability ratio, which stays qualitative (CLAIM-DIV-06). Also do not mix Acemoglu's two published versions in one chart (CLAIM-ECON-01), and do not put a headcount on UNESCO membership (CLAIM-GOV-05).
- **No WEF Future of Jobs Report 2026 exists.** The 170M/92M figures are from the 2025 edition. A blog recycling them under a 2026 date was found during research; do not follow it.
